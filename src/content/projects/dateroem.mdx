challenges:
  - title: Preventing fake payment confirmations
    problem: >-
      An M-Pesa callback is an ordinary HTTP request. If the system trusted it on its own, anyone
      who could reach the endpoint could mark an order as paid.
    approach: >-
      I never trust the callback alone. Each one is confirmed with Safaricom’s own transaction
      query before anything changes, and the order and payment updates are idempotent, so a
      repeated callback has no further effect.
    outcome: A forged callback cannot mark an order as paid, and duplicate callbacks are harmless.
  - title: One account, one phone
    problem: >-
      Staff use the field app on their own phones. A stolen or shared password should not be
      enough to open it.
    approach: >-
      Each phone holds its own key. At sign-in the app signs a one-time challenge from the server,
      and only phones an administrator has approved receive a session. The app also sits behind
      the fingerprint or screen lock.
    outcome: A password alone no longer opens the staff app; access is tied to approved phones.
  - title: Working with poor connectivity
    problem: Field staff record trees, trips and payments in places with weak or no signal.
    approach: >-
      The GPS pin works offline, M-Pesa messages read on the company SIM wait in a queue that
      syncs when the connection returns, and the dashboard keeps a copy for when the signal
      drops, with pull-to-reload once it is back.
    outcome: >-
      Payment records survive a weak signal, and staff can still see their dashboard when they
      are offline.
  - title: Three very different clients
    problem: >-
      A website, an Android app and a Qt desktop console each need data in a different shape, and
      a change for one should not break the others.
    approach: >-
      I used the backend-for-frontend pattern: one small FastAPI service per client shapes the
      data and handles sign-in limits and error wording, while the Spring Boot core keeps the
      business rules.
    outcome: Each client stays simple and can change on its own, with business rules in one place.
  - title: One brand across web, Android and desktop
    problem: The three apps use different toolkits but should feel like one product.
    approach: >-
      Colour tokens and assets are generated from one source, including the wood texture system
      and the emblem at every icon size. Per-account themes apply live, without restarting or
      signing in again.
    outcome: The apps look consistent, and a theme change applies immediately.
  - title: Keeping a small team able to run it
    problem: The people who run the system day to day are not developers.
    approach: >-
      Errors are written in plain language with no stack traces, a maintenance page appears when
      a service is down, and a desktop control panel starts and stops each service with live logs.
    outcome: Routine operations happen in the admin console rather than at a terminal.

## Problem and context

The business buys trees from farmers on buying trips, has them sawn and finished, and sells poles, sawn timber, boards, hardwood and furniture templates. Some orders are bulk (“heavy”) orders delivered by lorry.

Before this system, orders, trips, expenses, farmer payments and deliveries were tracked by hand. Customers paid by M-Pesa, but nothing reconciled those payments against orders.

## Goals

- Customers can browse the catalogue and pay online.
- Staff record trips, trees, expenses and deliveries from the field, sometimes offline.
- Administrators control users, money and servers from one place.

## My role

I was the sole developer. I gathered requirements with the business owner and board, then handled the architecture, implementation, security hardening, deployment and documentation. The system went live in October 2026, and in November I handed it over complete, with a go-live checklist of the tasks that remain the owner's.

## Architecture

<DateroemArchitecture />

- **Clients.** A React website for customers, an Android app (Kotlin, Jetpack Compose) for staff, and a desktop admin console (Python, PySide6/Qt) for administrators.
- **Backends-for-frontends.** Three small FastAPI services, one per client. Each shapes the data for its client, passes sign-in tokens through, limits sign-in attempts, turns errors into plain messages and handles media uploads.
- **Core API.** A Spring Boot (Java 17) REST backend with about 170 endpoints over MySQL 8. It owns the business rules, authentication, payments, SMS, email and AI features.
- **Edge.** A Caddy reverse proxy routes `/`, `/mobile-api/` and `/admin-api/`, and serves a friendly maintenance page when a service is down. Development ran on Ubuntu (WSL2) behind a secure tunnel. Since 25 October 2026 the system has run live on a Linux VPS under its own domain, dateroemwoods.co.ke, with systemd services, HTTPS through Caddy, and releases installed from checksummed packages.

## Key features

### Commerce

- Product catalogue with photos and videos, campaigns, a Featured banner, search, and ratings and reviews with consent and moderation.
- Cart and delivery-area picker with “near me” geolocation.
- Bulk-order enquiry page with an example-photo slideshow.
- Shop-by-type buttons built automatically from the product types, and sliding carousels for featured campaigns and delivery areas.
- Order tickets (DW-…) and a public “Track my order” page that needs only the ticket and the last three digits of the phone number.
- Returns, privacy and terms pages, with the AI assistant and FAQs following the same rules.

### Payments

- Safaricom M-Pesa (Daraja) STK Push checkout, live in production.
- Every payment callback is verified against Safaricom’s own transaction query, so a forged callback cannot mark an order as paid.
- Receipts stored per payment, and order status polled until confirmed.
- Admin payout flows with amount and recipient guards: a payout must go to the number on file unless the SuperAdmin approves.
- Limits on payment prompts per account, number and address, so the checkout cannot be used to flood someone's phone.

### Identity and security

- JWT authentication and BCrypt password hashing.
- Five roles (Customer, Employee, Marketer, Admin, SuperAdmin) enforced on the server, with a single protected SuperAdmin.
- Google Sign-In with server-side ID-token verification.
- Customer phone numbers verified by SMS one-time codes (Africa’s Talking). Only hashes are stored, with rate limits per number, per account and per hour.
- Device binding: the staff app signs a one-time server challenge with a per-phone key, and only approved phones get a session. The desktop console runs only on approved computers.
- A fingerprint or screen-lock gate in the Android app, and a sign-in cut-off after a data clean-up.
- Wrong-password limits, plus a code by email for accounts that have orders.
- A handover code on every order: goods are released only to someone who has it, and every handover is logged.
- A tamper-evident activity log: append-only, sealed with a hash chain, recording who did what with before and after values. Only the SuperAdmin can view or export it.
- Staff ID details encrypted at rest, open only to the SuperAdmin, and each opening logged.
- Account profiles, one-time temporary passwords on reset, and an idle lock on the desktop console.
- User-friendly errors everywhere, with no stack traces exposed.

### Field operations (Android)

- Buying trips, with one active trip across the whole app.
- Tree registration with GPS coordinates captured offline.
- Expenses, loans and capital recorded per trip.
- Heavy and light deliveries, confirmed with the customer’s handover code and an on-screen signature.
- Camera photo and video capture with on-device compression.
- M-Pesa SMS reading on the company SIM to reconcile payments made outside the app, with an offline queue that syncs later.
- A voice assistant using speech recognition and text-to-speech.
- Pull-to-reload, and a copy of the dashboard kept for when the signal drops.

### Administration (desktop)

- Dashboard and insights.
- Pending orders grouped per customer and area, with LATE and DUE SOON deadlines, sent to light or bulk deliveries on a trip.
- Company data for the SuperAdmin: API keys and passwords can be replaced from the console, each change confirmed with a code sent by email.
- Notices written on the desktop that appear under a bell on the website.
- User management: enable or disable accounts, and approve phones and computers.
- Products, marketing and campaign media, chosen from the PC and uploaded on save.
- Trips and finance, delivery areas, ratings moderation and an FAQ editor.
- A raw database browser for the SuperAdmin.
- A server control panel that starts and stops every service, with live logs.

### AI assistant

- A chat assistant on the website for customers.
- A role-aware assistant in the staff app that suggests actions for each user’s role. It runs server-side on the Anthropic Claude API, with a fallback provider.

### Experience and design

- One brand across all three apps: a painted wood texture system generated from code, a company emblem exported to every icon size, and a cinematic photographic entrance animation with drifting mist.
- User-selectable themes (Forest, Dark and Light), stored per account.
- Reduced-motion support and screen-reader labels.
- A scroll-driven “From the forest to your door” story page.

### Data

- About 40 tables and more than 40 versioned SQL migrations.
- Stored functions and procedures for trip profit, soft deletes and an audit log.

### Engineering practice

- Eighteen technical documents (architecture, API, database, operations, troubleshooting and a go-live checklist), generated and kept under version control.
- A secret scan and automated document checks.
- Manual end-to-end testing over USB on real Android hardware, plus headless-browser screenshots.
- A backup before every schema change.
- Search engine set-up: per-page titles, a sitemap, product structured data and a verified Google Search Console property.
- A security review at handover: fail2ban, a least-privilege database account, a Permissions-Policy header and scheduled restarts.
